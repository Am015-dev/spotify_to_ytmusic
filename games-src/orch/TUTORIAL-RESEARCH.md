# Tutorial research: teaching board games by playing

Scope: how tutorials teach by doing (real moves, real board, a goal, consequences) versus text popups and checklists.
Method: web search snippets plus project pages. Where I only saw a title or a snippet (not the full page), the entry says "(snippet)".
Gaps are listed at the end of the Sources section. All URLs below appeared in search results.
Repo rule: published board games are described, not named, and URLs that name one are withheld (34 sources keep their URL).

### Area 3. Learning research

39. **Worked-example effect** (Wikipedia, summarising Sweller et al.) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect
    Worked examples reduce extraneous load and free capacity for schema building; best for novices.
40. **Expertise reversal effect** (Wikipedia) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect
    Guidance that helps novices becomes redundant or harmful as expertise grows: remove it. Remedy is fading worked steps.
41. **Expertise Reversal Effect and Its Implications for Learner-Tailored Instruction** (Kalyuga 2007) https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf
    Primary-source review: adapt guidance to learner level; fade from worked examples to problem solving.
42. **Can retrieval practice be improved by scaffolding?** (Psychonomic Bulletin and Review, 2017) https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-017-1366-9
    Scaffolded retrieval can backfire if too easy; diminishing cues help. Supports hints that thin out.
43. **Retrieval-Based Learning review** (Karpicke, Purdue, 2025) https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2025/2025_Karpicke_Retrieval_Based_Learning_Review.pdf
    (snippet) Retrieving beats re-reading; gains require successful retrieval. Quiz-by-doing beats re-showing.

### Area 4. Data on tutorials and retention

44. **The Impact of Tutorials on Games of Varying Complexity** (Andersen et al., CHI 2012, project page) https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/
    8 tutorial designs, 3 games, 45,000+ players. Tutorials raised play time up to 29% in the most complex game, no significant gain in two simpler games.
45. **Same paper, PDF** https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/andersen2012tio.pdf

46. **Mobile retention benchmarks 2026** (GameAnalytics summary) https://investgame.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2026-01-20-Mobile_retention_benchmarks_2026.pdf
    (snippet) Median D1 about 22%, top 10% about 40%; D7 median about 4%; many studios cut projects below 35% D1; value must show in the first 5 to 15 minutes.

## Synthesis

### The 10 rules of a great board-game tutorial

1. The tutorial is the game: real moves on the real board, with consequences, never a slideshow about it. [2,3,5,7,8,11,29,30]
2. One rule (or one verb) per scenario, with one visible objective; introduce the next rule only after the last is used. [1,7,9,12,13,24,29]
3. Constrain the board, not the player's attention: remove or pre-solve everything unrelated so the right move is discoverable and the wrong move is cheap. [7,12,13,30,32,39]
4. Teach by consequence: the move happens, the board visibly changes (score, resources, legality), and a single line says why. [3,7,8,12,33]
5. Failure is safe, instant and explained: one-tap retry, nothing lost, and the reason for failure is shown in context. [7,33,27,42]
6. Just-in-time beats up-front: show a rule at the moment it matters, never before the first move. [6,12,16,17,45]
7. Fade the help: start with worked examples (a shown, pre-played move), move to hints, then to nothing, as the player shows skill. [16,39,40,41,42]
8. Match length to rules weight: a few minutes for light games; split heavy games into per-concept or per-faction pieces; do not run an hour of lecture. [18,24,26,36,44]
9. Always skippable, replayable and resumable, with a rules reference and on-demand help reachable from play; tutorial is not the only teacher. [6,27,34,31,15]
10. End on a real game against a competent but gentle opponent, and let players practise retrieval (do it unaided) rather than re-reading. [29,30,32,35,43,33]

Supporting evidence of effect size is thin and mixed: tutorials clearly matter for complex games and little for simple ones [44,45]; first-session experience is tied to D1 retention [46,47].

### Anti-patterns

- Text wall before the first move: working memory is overloaded and players skip it [5,12,15,39].
- Forced click-the-glow with everything else locked: teaches obedience, not the rule; players learn "click the highlight", not why [2,3,12].
- Modal dimming that covers the board: the thing being taught is hidden, and the player cannot look at the consequence [3,17].
- Long lecture before the first move, or teaching every rule before turn one: front-loaded information outlasts patience and is forgotten before use [12,18,45].
- Tutorial that cannot be skipped or replayed: experts get punished (expertise reversal) and novices cannot revisit [6,40,41].
- Vague scripted steps with a better rules page hidden elsewhere: players end up reading the reference anyway [27].
- Failing without explanation, or failing with a heavy penalty: no learning signal, and retries feel costly [5,7,33].
- Teaching only the first turn then dropping the player: mid-game rules never get introduced [31].
- Hints that never fade, or that hand over the answer every time: removes retrieval effort and stalls mastery [40,42,43].
- Not telling players how the opponent behaves: players cannot learn what to expect [28].
- Funny or heavy narration without brevity: charm helps but does not replace a short path to play [18,19,29].
- Gating the game behind sign-up or long setup before the first lesson: first minutes decide retention [32,46].

### Recommended structure for our single-file browser board games

1. **6 to 10 scripted mini-scenarios, one rule each.**
   - Each scenario: a constrained pre-built board position, a single objective stated in one line, the real move UI with only relevant legal moves enabled, immediate visible feedback, one-tap instant retry on failure with the reason shown, a one-line debrief naming the rule just learnt, and a par/stars rating (fewest moves or a score target) for replay value.
   - Why: scenarios are the board-game equivalent of a short test chamber or World 1-1: one idea, a safe space, a test of it [7,9,10,13]. Constraining the board is the load-reduction that makes novices succeed [39,12]. Instant retry plus explanation makes failure the teacher [33,7]. Per-faction or per-concept splits suit heavy or asymmetric games [24,18]. Par/stars give a reason to replay, which doubles as retrieval practice [43,42].
   - Rule ordering: place early scenarios as worked examples (show the solution move as a ghost on request), later ones as open problems, fading help as in [41].
   - Keep each scenario to about 1 to 2 minutes; the whole set about 10 to 15 minutes for a mid-weight game, 3 to 5 for a light one [26,36,18].

2. **A guided real game against an easy AI with fading hints.**
   - Full rules, real scoring, a gentle opponent. Hints appear just-in-time (when a rule first becomes relevant or the player stalls), then thin out; first game may use a simplified rule subset as one big-name digital card game does [32].
   - Why: the bridge from drilled rules to real decisions is where reviewers say tutorials stop too early [31], and fading is the research-backed way to move from worked examples to independent play [40,41]. A competent but not cruel opponent keeps the lesson safe; strong AI is a later reward [35]. Say how the opponent tends to play [28].

3. **Free play, on-demand help and a rules reference.**
   - Always-available "what can I do here?" legal-move hints, a searchable one-page rules reference with the scenario list for re-entry, undo where the rules allow, and all of it skippable from the start.
   - Why: experts should not be forced through guidance [6,40]; a reference beats vague scripted steps for lookup [27,34]; players returning after a gap need re-entry points. Games whose mechanics can be discovered need little tutorial [44,45], so keep the mandatory part short and everything else optional.

Measurement note: judge the tutorial by whether a fresh player makes a legal, sensible first real-game move unaided, and by completion of scenario 1 and the guided game, not by self-report. Public funnel data is scarce [46,47]; our own per-scenario retry and quit counts are the real evidence.
